# Decision-oriented reports

No bullet or numbered lists anywhere in report.md. Use prose for interpretation and tables for comparisons, findings, risks, contradictions, evidence gaps, questions and recommendations. Keep tables readable on an A4 page: at most two text columns: a short item title and details. Put explanation, evidence citations, consequences, next steps and uncertainty inside Details, using brief labelled phrases where helpful. Findings, risks and issues may add up to three narrow categorical estimate columns (such as impact, likelihood or confidence), with translated Low / Medium / High values only when justified. Keep all words intact; do not force arbitrary line breaks into words. Use one issue per row. Every data row carries its own evidence citations. Use stable issue identifiers to cross-reference related findings and actions without duplicating narrative.

Choose a meaningful grouping from the actual material: decision impact, urgency, review criterion, business area or evidence strength. Explain the grouping and ordering briefly. Prioritize by stated, evidence-grounded consequences; use Critical / High / Moderate / Low only when justified, otherwise “Not assessable”. Do not invent likelihood scores, numeric risk metrics or owners. Separate impact from confidence: uncertain evidence does not imply low risk. Distinguish reported contradictions from proven errors.

A risks table uses Issue and Details, optionally adding narrow categorical estimates. Recommendations use Action and Details; include responsible roles or timing only when sourced, otherwise mark them proposed. Questions should connect each question to the decision it resolves. Missing-evidence rows should identify the requested record and why it matters. Fit the chosen structure to the project, rather than applying every possible column.

If the skill template calls for a decision summary, give that chapter a compact, cited decision overview and a meaningful visual when data permits. Derive an elegant horizontal bar chart, grouped dot plot or priority-by-theme matrix from the classified table rows to show the distribution of issues at a glance. Count each stable issue ID once, state the denominator and include unassessed issues explicitly. Counts describe this review's findings, not the probability or prevalence of real-world harm. Show numeric comparisons or intervals instead when they answer the decision better. If there are too few items for a useful chart, use a compact summary table and explain the limitation.

Charts must reconcile with the tables and cited CSV data, use restrained colors and direct labels, and retain readable legends and captions in the PDF. Follow charts.md for accessible palettes, chart selection and evidence rules. Do not manufacture a chart merely to fill space.
